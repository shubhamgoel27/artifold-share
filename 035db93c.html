The Math Behind Dobble

Why does each card have 8 symbols? Why are there 57 different symbols? And why do every two cards share exactly one — never zero, never two? It's not a clever printing trick. It's a 150-year-old piece of geometry.

🃏 55 cards 🎨 57 distinct symbols 🔢 8 symbols per card 🤝 Any 2 cards share exactly 1

The puzzle, in one question

You want to design a deck of cards where:

  • every card has the same number of symbols, call it k
  • any two cards share exactly one symbol

Given k, what's the most cards you can possibly make? And how many distinct symbols do you need? Let's build up to it.

2Where does the formula come from?

Pick any single card C from the deck. Say it has the k symbols s₁, s₂, …, sₖ. Now look at every other card.

Each one must share exactly one symbol with C. So we can drop every other card into one of k buckets, based on which of C's symbols it shares:

Inside one bucket, all cards already share C's symbol sᵢ. They can't share anything else too — that would break the rule. So among the k − 1 "other" symbols on each card, every symbol can appear at most once across the whole bucket.

The non-sᵢ symbols available are everything not on C — that's V − k symbols (where V is the total). Each card in the bucket eats up k − 1 distinct ones. So the bucket holds at most (V − k)/(k − 1) cards.

Now look across buckets. Pick one card from bucket 1 and one from bucket 2 — they share different symbols of C, so their one mandatory shared symbol must come from outside C. Working through this consistency forces:

Each bucket holds exactly k − 1 cards.
Total cards = 1 (the card C itself) + k buckets × (k − 1) cards each

Nmax = k² − k + 1

A symmetric argument (swap "card" and "symbol") gives the same count for distinct symbols. So:

With k symbols per card, the maximum deck has k² − k + 1 cards drawn from k² − k + 1 distinct symbols.

4Why this is really projective geometry

The system above has a name: a finite projective plane of order q, where q = k − 1. Translate the words:

Dobble language

  • card
  • symbol
  • two cards share exactly one symbol
  • each symbol appears on the same number of cards

Geometry language

  • line
  • point
  • two lines meet at exactly one point
  • each point lies on the same number of lines

In ordinary geometry, two parallel lines never meet. Projective planes fix this by adding "points at infinity" — every pair of lines crosses, somewhere. Finite projective planes are the same idea, but with a finite number of points.

The catch: not every k works

A finite projective plane of order q is known to exist when q is a prime power (2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11, 13, 16, …). For other q, the answer is sometimes "no" and sometimes still open:

Order qk = q+1Status
2, 3, 4, 5, 73, 4, 5, 6, 8✅ Exists (prime / prime power)

8, 99, 10✅ Exists (prime power)

So a Dobble-style deck with 7 symbols per card is not just hard to design — it's provably impossible. The closest workable values are k = 6 (gives 31 cards) and k = 8 (gives 57). Eight is the prettiest size that's still playable in your hand. That's why Dobble is what it is.

Recap

Dobble is a finite projective plane of order 7 (k=8 symbols per card). The "exactly one shared symbol" property is what mathematicians call incidence geometry. The formula N = k² − k + 1 drops out of a clean counting argument.

The choice of k = 8 isn't arbitrary — it sits at the sweet spot where the geometry exists, the deck is a reasonable size (57), and a card fits in your hand. k = 7 was off the table because the underlying geometry literally cannot exist.
